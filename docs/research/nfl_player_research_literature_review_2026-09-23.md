**My assessment is that the system has a strong evidence foundation, but its next useful advance is estimating player ability and opportunity under changing conditions.** We already have extensive descriptive history, chronological experiments, market comparisons, and unusually explicit records of failed hypotheses. The most promising next research is sample-aware estimation of player strengths, role-dependent workload, and calibrated outcome distributions. Another broad inventory of loosely related statistics is a lower priority.

That is a research judgment, not a finding that these proposed methods will outperform our existing models. External evidence supports the questions and methods; our own properly matched tests must establish their usefulness here.

**Scope and how to read the evidence.** This is a targeted critical literature review, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Searches covered NFL player attribution, receiving opportunity, rushing and passing expectations, college translation, aging, availability measurement, forecast uncertainty, model selection, and fantasy decision utility. I prioritized original papers, author manuscripts, research published by the organizations that produced the data, and official data documentation. Soccer studies and generic player-prediction repositories were excluded from NFL evidence. General statistical papers are identified as methodological evidence rather than NFL results.

The emphasis is QB/RB/WR/TE because those are the populations in the current analysis engine. Offensive-line and defensive evaluation appear as a longer-term extension. This does not establish a complete scouting system for all NFL positions. Practitioner research, particularly PFF and ESPN, is useful but often depends on proprietary charting and is less independently reproducible than open methods. Correlation, descriptive attribution, future prediction, and acquisition value receive different interpretations throughout.

I inspected the current catalog, published registry and evaluation artifacts, relevant source code, and the latest internal research reports. Existing numerical results below are readouts of those artifacts, not newly replicated experiments. Documentation describing previous releases is treated as historical when it conflicts with the current catalog. Source availability was checked online on the review date; an upstream description does not certify completeness of our particular capture.

**What the system actually serves today.** The authority is [the current catalog](../../data/current.json), which selects corrected gold `canonical_nextgen_20260923_r1`, profiles `nextgen_products_20260923_r2_profiles`, and analysis `nextgen_system_20260923_r5`. The [published registry](../../data/research/nextgen_system_20260923_r5/registry.json) contains 1,626 entries: 28 approved descriptive definitions, 13 reference baselines, 26 shadow entries, and 1,559 archive entries. The [release report](../../data/research/nextgen_system_20260923_r5/report.json) covers 839 current candidates and 13 forecast targets.

The normal product currently serves observations and explicitly labelled reference estimates. The reference forecast carries forward last-season observations, adjusts totals for nominal season length, or uses an earlier position/population mean when history is absent. Appearance probability uses an earlier population frequency. Ridge and histogram boosting remain research challengers. These are preseason estimates, not current rest-of-season projections. The observation capture is through 2026 Week 2; completed-season tracking ends in 2025. See [the implementation record](../history/nextgen_system_implementation_2026-09-23.md) and [the actual forecast code](../../src/patron/metrics/nextgen.py).

This distinction matters: some older documents discuss V2, Adaptive, four-week outlooks, or roster valuations as live capabilities. Their saved results do not describe current serving behavior. Conversely, withholding their predictive claims does not erase valid observations or invalidate every result in the archives.

**The literature’s first lesson is to define what a “strength” means.** A player can have high observed output because of ability, deployment, teammates, opponents, or favorable events. He can also possess a useful skill while receiving little opportunity to demonstrate it. A profile should therefore distinguish these questions:

| Question | Suitable evidence | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What did the player produce? | Audited counts, rates, participation, and distributions | Isolated ability or future production |
| What can the player do in a given situation? | Context-adjusted, sample-aware execution estimates | What role a coach will give him |
| What opportunities will he receive? | Team volume, role probabilities, competition, availability, deployment | Efficiency conditional on those opportunities |
| What will he produce next? | Joint forecasts of opportunity and execution, with uncertainty | Whether his acquisition price is attractive |
| What should our fantasy team do? | Forecasts plus roster constraints, alternatives, prices, and decision objective | A universally correct player ranking |

The existing separation of measurements, predictions, and decisions is worth preserving. A further distinction would help within measurements: directly counted observations versus model-derived measurements. EPA, CPOE, and yards over expected incorporate a fitted expectation model even when displayed retrospectively.

**Player attribution supports partial pooling, with substantial limits on causal claims.** Yurko, Ventura, and Horowitz’s [nflWAR paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.00998), published in JQAS in 2019, uses multilevel models to allocate offensive contributions while accounting for elements of teammate and opponent context. Its estimates pull players with fewer observations toward group averages and include resampling uncertainty. The authors explicitly acknowledge missing information about individual offensive linemen, schemes, and coaching. This is a strong methodological precedent for conditional player estimates; it is not proof that a statistical residual identifies intrinsic talent or predicts fantasy points.

For Patron, this suggests testing a player-strength layer in which a rate carries its exposure, context, and uncertainty. The project already uses ridge regularization and, in the archived QB role experiment, fixed efficiency shrinkage. The genuinely new addition would be coherent pooling by player, role, and context, with shrinkage strength learned from earlier data. It would not be accurate to describe shrinkage itself as absent from the repository.

A simple illustration is a binomial skill estimate: after observing `s` successes in `n` eligible opportunities, a beta prior gives an estimated rate `(s + alpha) / (n + alpha + beta)`. The prior must match the task and be estimated without future observations. More complex hierarchical models can distinguish positions, deployment, and eras. Twenty opportunities and two hundred opportunities should not provide equally precise evidence of the same observed rate. Dependence among plays also means raw exposure is not automatically an independent sample size.

**Expected-value metrics are useful comparisons, not measurement truth.** [Brill, Yee, Deshpande, and Wyner (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04889v1) examine selection bias, dependence, uncertainty, and overfitting in expected-points estimation. In particular, a randomly sampled play can overrepresent stronger offenses; its expectation is not automatically the expectation for an average team. The available manuscript is an early preprint. Its contribution is a concrete warning about the estimand and model assumptions, not evidence that every existing EPA result is invalid.

The related [win-probability simulation by Brill, Yurko, and Wyner (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16171v3) finds that clustered observations can substantially reduce effective information and that even cluster-bootstrap intervals can undercover in their simulated setting. We should not copy their numerical correction into season-level player forecasts: our sampling structure is different. We should test our own uncertainty procedure under repeated players, shared teams, overlapping horizons, and era changes.

Our application should identify which expectation model produced an imported statistic, its version when available, and its historical reconstruction status. A feature observed in an earlier season can still embed a later-trained or revised provider model. This is distinct from ordinary future-row leakage. If an upstream training vintage is unavailable, label the result retrospective rather than asserting that exact feature value was available at the original decision date. [Baldwin’s nflfastR model documentation](https://opensourcefootball.com/posts/2020-09-28-nflfastr-ep-wp-and-cp-models/) describes separate EP, WP, completion, xYAC, and pass-probability models; their outputs should retain their own identities.

**Receiving research favors examining the opportunity process.** PFF’s original [route-level receiving study](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-best-wide-receivers-by-route-type-rob-gronkowski-michael-thomas-george-kittle) models target probability using route, alignment, depth, coverage, and situation. It reports that targets above expectation predict next-year adjusted yards per route with an R-squared of about 0.25, versus about 0.08 for yards per target. These are results from PFF’s definitions and sample, not portable coefficients for our models.

Barrett’s [2019 YPRR analysis](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-the-fantasy-value-of-yards-per-route-run) reports a 0.43 correlation with next-season receiving fantasy points, but selects receivers with at least 50 targets in both seasons. That future-season eligibility condition limits applicability to preseason candidates who may lose their jobs or disappear. The study motivates a test; it does not justify dropping our zero-production outcomes.

ESPN evaluates untargeted routes and separates openness, catching, and YAC. [Burke, 2022](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores).

For our system, the proposed receiving profile is a sequence: participation in passing plays, actual route running, target earning conditional on routes, catch execution conditional on target difficulty, and production after the catch. Each stage needs its own denominator. On a consistently defined observed sample, these components can explain how receiving production arose. Forecasting them separately and multiplying their marginal expectations would not preserve dependence; a conditional or joint model is needed.

An illustrative comparison: two receivers each receive 60 targets, one on 200 routes and the other on 400. Their target rates are 30% and 15%, suggesting different questions about earning targets and receiving more deployment. It does not establish that the first is twice as talented: route type, quarterback decisions, and defensive attention remain relevant. This kind of explanation is more useful than treating every correlated receiving metric as another independent endorsement.

The present `route_opportunities` measure counts passing-play participation. It is not verified routes run. That is already recognized in the code and documentation. NFL Next Gen Stats average separation is measured at catch or incompletion, so it cannot describe every route or every moment at which a receiver was available. Our [tracking definitions](../../src/patron/metrics/profile_tracking.py) correctly avoid calling it isolated talent. The [provider dictionary](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/dictionary_nextgen_stats.html) supports this interpretation.

**QB evaluation needs separate passing, rushing, and job components.** Completion execution, target choice, pocket outcomes, designed rushing, and scrambling answer different questions. CPOE should accompany its pass sample and model definition; it does not directly measure the value of the target chosen. Deshpande and Evans’s [Expected Hypothetical Completion Probability](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.12337) estimates completion possibilities for alternative receivers and propagates uncertainty about unobserved hypothetical inputs. Its dataset covers 91 games in the first six weeks of 2017. It offers a framework for studying decisions, not a broad longitudinal forecast validation. Maximizing completion probability alone is also not the same objective as maximizing play value.

Our practical research should begin with what we can observe: attempts per relevant game or role, designed carries versus scrambles, first-down production, and context-adjusted passing execution. Add sack/dropback and pressure-conditioned analyses only with consistent definitions and coverage. Pressure splits themselves contain selection effects: protection, route timing, opponent behavior, and quarterback actions jointly determine pressure. We should avoid attaching all pressure-related changes to one player.

The most informative local failure is already in [the QB job experiment](qb_role_transition_2026-09-23.md). It recognized Lamar Jackson’s 2019 opening role much better after dated evidence, yet the starter expert predicted only 24.28 season attempts. Better probability on that expert made the opportunity estimate worse. This directly motivates pooling newly promoted players toward plausible workloads in the new role and separately estimating time spent in that role. Another starter/news indicator alone would not address the documented failure.

**RB evaluation should distinguish opportunity allocation from rushing execution.** A useful profile separately describes carry share, passing-game involvement, short-yardage/end-zone work, and performance conditional on rushing context. A single yards-per-carry ranking cannot identify why production occurred. The original [PFF rushing report](https://media.pff.com/2018/02/17rushing.pdf) studies rushing statistics and their relationship with later team performance; that team-level target is different from predicting an individual back’s fantasy scoring.

Yurko and colleagues’ [Going Deep](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01760) estimates within-play ball-carrier outcomes using player trajectories and extends the approach to outcome distributions. The transferable idea is conditioning on the state of the play and retaining more than its mean. The current gold tables contain season-level NGS RYOE, not the tracking trajectories required to reproduce that model.

A sensible near-term hypothesis is that shrunk rushing execution helps describe strengths while role and team opportunity dominate much of the forecastable volume. Whether RYOE predicts future workload, future rushing efficiency, or future fantasy points must be tested separately. Our family audit has only four admitted RYOE evaluation years, 2022–2025, and no clear increment after ECR. That is a narrow result about the tested preseason model, not a verdict that rushing ability is unmeasurable. See [the signal audit](nextgen_signal_consolidation_2026-09-23.md).

**TE needs its own deployment interpretation.** Total snaps can contain substantial blocking work. Therefore, treating offensive snap share as receiving opportunity can be especially misleading. The proposed TE analysis should distinguish pass-game deployment, target earning, alignment, and execution when the underlying evidence permits it. Small route-type samples need pooling rather than dozens of independent rankings. Position changes and hybrid roles should be explicit support checks, given the documented extrapolation problems in our profile models. These are recommendations based on our definitions and failure cases, not established improvements from a completed TE experiment.

**College translation, draft capital, and athleticism address different populations.** [Lyons and colleagues (2011)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08959285.2011.555218?journalCode=hhup20) report stronger relationships between collegiate performance and later NFL performance than between several combine tests and later performance. The accessible publisher abstract supports that limited statement; it does not establish an increment after draft capital for our scoring target.

[Szekely and colleagues (2023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05774) distinguish reaching NFL snaps from subsequent snap production. Their reported success is much weaker for the latter. Their complete-case selection reduces 1,973 records to 805, and their stated 80/20 split is not a demonstrated rolling temporal evaluation. I would give this study limited weight for production adoption while retaining its useful separation of outcomes.

Our finding that draft capital is a strong rookie baseline is compatible with college performance being informative. Draft decisions already summarize scouting, production, athleticism, and expected opportunity. Conditioning on draft capital asks whether we add information after those decisions; it does not estimate college production’s total relationship with NFL success. For pre-draft scouting, final draft position is unavailable and must be excluded. For post-draft fantasy forecasting, it is an essential comparison. Those should be separate research tracks.

The current [college pathway work](../history/college_nfl_pathways_2026-09-22.md) already handles identity review, missing history, and maturation of three-year outcomes. Its remaining substantive gap is an independently verified college-exit population: failure to match an NFL identity cannot be treated as proof of failure to reach the NFL. Competition, team context, age at production, transfer history, and deployment are reasonable hypotheses, but another raw college-total score has weak support as the next priority.

**Age and availability should be modeled as processes with selection.** Riske’s [PFF aging analysis](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-investigating-positional-aging-curves-with-pff-war) demonstrates how the mix of positions and surviving players can distort age-performance summaries. Its use of aggregate WAR by age is useful for examining roster-level patterns; it is not a fully identified within-player biological aging curve. Our recommendation is to separate changing performance conditional on participation from the probability of retaining a meaningful role, while retaining exits and known zero production. Preserve older history, but test era adjustment and recency weighting inside training folds rather than assuming every historical observation is equally applicable.

For availability, the NFL-specific [public-data validation study (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34166138/) compares published injury studies with league medical surveillance. Public studies captured an average of 66% of recorded ACL injuries, with capture varying by position and player visibility. This is evidence about ascertainment, not a general prediction accuracy figure or an injury-risk estimate for any player.

Patron’s distinction between scoring appearances and medical availability is therefore important. A future availability model should separate roster eligibility, recorded active/inactive status, role participation, and documented injury-related absence when labels permit. Current news capture and reviewed absences are valuable source work; they do not constitute a validated medical model. The existing [current-injury capture](../history/current_injury_capture_2026-09-23.md) is a separate raw snapshot and is not automatically incorporated into the published gold forecasts.

**Prediction distributions would add capability that descriptive volatility cannot supply.** A historical weekly standard deviation describes variation under past roles and circumstances. An interval for the next game or season must also account for uncertainty about the future role, team opportunity, and model parameters. Likewise, a confidence interval on average model improvement is not an individual player prediction interval.

[Gneiting and Raftery (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437) provide the foundation for evaluating probability distributions with proper scoring rules. For our application, retain Brier scores for events and consider CRPS or appropriate quantile/interval scores for continuous outcomes, alongside interval coverage and width. Evaluate calibration by position, role certainty, and sample size rather than relying only on a pooled figure. This is a methodological recommendation, not NFL-specific evidence that a particular distribution will fit well.

A second issue is the meaning of a point forecast. [Gneiting’s point-forecast analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0902) explains why loss functions must match the requested functional: squared error targets the conditional mean; absolute error targets a conditional median. Our ridge and default boosting challengers fit squared-error objectives but are primarily screened using MAE, with an MSE guardrail. That is a legitimate comparison of point forecasts, but it should not be interpreted as directly selecting the best expected-value forecast. For sparse or skewed player outcomes, publish mean and median distinctly if both matter. An illustrative 60% chance of zero and 40% chance of 100 has mean 40 and median zero; these answer different questions.

Conformal methods are a possible calibration wrapper, not a shortcut around time dependence. [Gibbs and Candès (2024)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/22-1218.html) develop online prediction sets under distribution shifts. Applying such methods here would require chronological calibration, handling delayed/overlapping labels, and checking subgroup behavior. Long-run coverage does not guarantee coverage for one particular injured player or new starter. Begin with a simple honest distributional benchmark before adding an adaptive method.

**Fantasy utility depends on the actual decision.** Massey and Thaler’s [2013 NFL draft study](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/massey---thaler---losers-curse---management-science-july-2013.pdf) finds early picks overvalued relative to expected surplus value. [Brill and Wyner’s later analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10400v3) shows how a utility emphasizing elite outcomes can change that interpretation. This is a useful disagreement: the preferred choice can depend on what the decision maker values. It is not evidence that our fantasy roster should mechanically prefer greater variance.

[Haugh and Singal](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3528) explicitly model contest payouts, opponents, and feasible DFS lineups. The paper supports evaluating a decision policy in its actual environment; its daily contests are not our season-long ESPN league. For Patron, acquisition and lineup tests must reflect our full-PPR rules, big-play bonuses, roster limits, replacement options, and decision-time information. Our existing refusal to equate lower MAE with a market advantage is correct.

**Several data constraints change the priority order.** The [official nflverse availability schedule](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/nflverse_data_schedule.html) says the pre-2023 NGS participation source ended during 2023; newer FTN participation is released after the postseason rather than live. It also reports that the injury source ended after 2024 and describes timestamped depth-chart updates from 2025. These are upstream constraints, not proof that every local record from those periods is invalid. Any alternative/backfilled source needs its own provenance.

The [participation dictionary](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/dictionary_participation.html) defines its route field for the primary receiver. It does not provide a complete labelled route for every eligible receiver. Therefore, we cannot obtain a full live YPRR dataset simply by renaming a participation column. Historical in-season studies using postseason-released data also need an availability audit; filtering to the game date is insufficient. Preseason use in the following year is a different timing question.

Our NGS adapter selects four season aggregates and discards accompanying exposure fields. The provider schema includes attempts, targets/receptions, and rushing attempts, so there is a concrete opportunity to investigate preserving provider-matched counts. Those counts must be reconciled to the exact statistic’s eligible sample; the presence of a total in the same file does not prove it is the correct denominator for every tracking measure. See [the adapter](../../src/patron/metrics/enrichment.py) and [the NGS dictionary](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/dictionary_nextgen_stats.html).

Expected fantasy points are also not wholly new to the repo: archived enrichment uses them and current gold excludes modeled xFP inputs. [ffopportunity](https://ffopportunity.ffverse.com/) describes models trained on 2006–2020 public play-by-play. Reusing those outputs as if their fitted model existed at every earlier forecast date would be a different claim from retrospective opportunity measurement. A new study should use fold-specific fits where feasible, pin scoring definitions, and retain event-level information needed for this league’s bonuses. Importing a generic xFP total would not settle those questions.

**My proposed research order is below.** These are experiments to consider, not an implementation plan or approval to change the served system. Each adds a capability beyond the existing inventory or addresses an observed failure.

| Priority | Research question | Concrete comparison | Evidence needed before adoption |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Can sample-aware estimates distinguish repeatable strengths from noisy rates? | Earlier-data empirical-Bayes/multilevel estimates versus raw rates and existing ridge/boosting baselines; start with a small set of defined passing, rushing, and receiving components | Future component accuracy, uncertainty calibration, robustness across exposure bands and team changes; fantasy lift only if that is the intended use |
| 2 | Can role changes produce plausible new workloads? | Conditional role/workload model versus direct regression, prior usage, and the archived QB mixture; opening job, retention, and availability evaluated separately | Better calibrated role probabilities and conditional attempts/carries/targets, including low-prior-volume promotions; no recurrence of implausible starter workloads |
| 3 | Which receiving-opportunity components add information? | A small prespecified WR/TE comparison of validated participation, target earning, and deployment; use verified routes only when available | Gain beyond strong own-data baselines; separate ECR comparison for market claims; denominator and source-timing checks |
| 4 | Can we forecast useful ranges and event probabilities? | Simple distributional baseline versus quantile/hurdle or conditional-simulation challenger; explicit mean and median | Proper scores, coverage and width, subgroup calibration, and realistic zero/tail behavior on untouched outcomes |
| 5 | Can team context improve coherent player forecasts? | Conditional team opportunity plus within-team allocation versus independent player forecasts; include an other/unassigned category | Predictive gain and coherent totals; assess shared uncertainty rather than only marginal errors |
| 6 | Does development information add value at the appropriate stage? | Pre-draft college model without draft position; post-draft model against draft capital; future participation and execution as separate outcomes | Verified exit populations, matured outcomes, identity/coverage sensitivity, and temporal validation |
| 7 | Does any approved forecasting improvement change a real decision? | A fixed legal-lineup or acquisition policy using decision-time rosters, alternatives, and prices | Incremental policy value under the actual league rules, with prospective evaluation and complete candidate retention |

The first three should be a small coherent program. They share the question of whether a player’s observed rate represents a repeatable strength, a favorable assignment, or a role that may change. The fourth should accompany whichever prediction model is tested, rather than be postponed until after a ranking is marketed as confident. Priorities five through seven need additional data contracts and should not become excuses to enlarge the first experiment indefinitely.

For team allocation, the proposed conditional structure matters. Team dropbacks, targeted attempts, player target shares, and catch outcomes must use compatible totals; sacks and throwaways need explicit treatment. Allocating targets within a team can make teammates compete conditional on fixed volume, while shared game conditions can move both upward. Independent marginal forecasts will not reproduce all of those relationships. The recommendation is a test of coherent joint forecasts, not a claim that any particular Dirichlet or simulation recipe has been validated here.

For role evolution, a small state model could distinguish established role, competition/rotation, promotion, and absence, with separate conditional workload estimates. Time in a role is not equivalent to opening-day status. Begin with interpretable transitions and simple baselines; a hidden-state model is only justified if its latent states have useful, stable interpretation and improve the declared outcome. News can inform a transition probability, but a missing news record cannot stand for no transition.

**The evaluation program should become narrower and more decision-specific.** The current exhaustive screen is valuable as an inventory and filter. The next study should preregister a small number of hypotheses, populations, horizons, and losses, then perform all tuning and feature selection within earlier training periods. [Cawley and Talbot (2010)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html) show that overfitting the model-selection criterion can produce apparent gains comparable to differences among algorithms. Reusing the same historical years to propose and confirm successive improvements does not create independent evidence.

I would preserve the existing expanding chronological folds, fixed-era reports, paired comparisons, zero outcomes, and exact artifacts. Add these refinements to the next research protocol:

1. Declare whether the target is an expected total, typical outcome, rate conditional on exposure, role event, or decision payoff. Match the primary loss to that quantity; retain complementary diagnostics without changing the winner after seeing them.
2. Compare on the same cutoff-safe cases, including fallback behavior. Use a strong lean baseline, the existing fixed booster, and a market-informed baseline only where the claim concerns information beyond the market.
3. Report full-population results and prespecified decision-relevant cohorts based on information available at cutoff. A large group of low-output candidates can hide errors among plausible starters. Do not define success cohorts using future targets or games.
4. Separate player-weighted and exposure-weighted efficiency evaluations. They answer different questions; neither should silently replace the other. Retain small samples with honest uncertainty instead of deleting them using future exposure.
5. Evaluate dependence sensitivity. Season resampling is an appropriate improvement over independent player-row resampling for shared seasonal shocks, but repeated careers and few modern years remain concerns. Use suitable player/team/block sensitivity analyses or simulations without assuming one bootstrap solves every dependence structure.
6. Stress-test source changes, missingness, newly promoted players, team changes, position changes, and extreme predictions. Missingness indicators can encode provider era or player visibility rather than a football mechanism.
7. Treat historical comparisons as exploratory after repeated use. For new weekly hypotheses, freeze predictions before future outcome windows. For a new preseason claim, the next untouched preseason cohort is needed; the already-started 2026 season cannot become an untouched preseason holdout. Preserve the existing frozen 2026 contract separately.
8. Specify promotion and stopping rules before the new evaluation. More statistics, a successful fit, or a positive unadjusted slice should not automatically change serving policy. Conversely, a descriptive measure does not need a fantasy-market gain to remain useful.

**What I would not prioritize now.** I would not build another universal player score, stack every family that once showed a favorable interval, add deterministic volatility penalties, or infer medical risk from scoring appearances. I would not make unrestricted linear career-volume models the default given the archived Michael Robinson and Kendall Hinton extrapolations. A bounded learner helps with those examples but does not by itself resolve support or calibration. I also would not treat complex tracking networks as a near-term replacement for the current data foundation.

There is worthwhile longer-term research for broader NFL evaluation. [Chu and colleagues’ route identification](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.02423) uses trajectory clustering to recover deployment patterns. [Nguyen, Yurko, and Matthews’s STRAIN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10262) measures pass-rush pressure through distance and closing speed and models player effects. [Yurko, Nguyen, and Pelechrinis’s NFL Ghosts](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17220v2) compares observed defender positioning with conditional reference distributions. These offer concrete ways to examine contributions missed by box scores, but they require tracking inputs absent from our current gold. Their attribution models also do not supply automatic causal counterfactuals or fantasy forecasts.

**The useful end product would be a player evidence profile with explicit unresolved questions.** For example, a receiver profile could state that target earning is high for his documented deployment, distinguish that estimate from his current route opportunity, show the sample and uncertainty, and identify whether the next forecast depends mostly on increased participation or improved execution. A QB profile could show confident opening-role evidence alongside uncertainty about retention and conditional rushing volume. This would support actual player understanding while remaining compatible with the existing claim-specific serving policy.

The recommended next research objective is therefore concrete: determine whether a small, sample-aware, position-specific model of opportunity and execution improves the corresponding football outcomes and their uncertainty. Preserve the current evidence discipline, use the strongest existing baseline, and require a separate test before turning any gain into a claim of fantasy value.
